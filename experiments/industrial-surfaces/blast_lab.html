Blast profile lab

Paint over grit-blasted steel. On the left and in the middle is a slow simulation of the real process: angular grit pits the steel (faceted craters with raised rims, several impacts deep), then primer and topcoat level the profile, and the result is rendered with 4×4 supersampling. On the right is a fast kernel that works out each pixel on its own, with no bitmap. It is fitted to the painted reference by matching the luminance mean and its spread in three frequency bands. All sizes are physical, in millimetres.

Model

Steel. Grit particles of diameter d (±40 %) land at random until each point has been hit about coverage times. Each impact cuts a pyramidal pit with three to five facets, radius 0.22d and depth 0.35 of its radius, stretched up to 1.6× along a random direction (impacts are oblique). It also pushes up a rim. The surface is the lower envelope of all the pits, which gives the sharp ridges where pits meet. At 0.5 mm grit the depth comes out at about 40 µm, inside the 40–100 µm anchor-profile range given for blasted steel.

Paint. Each wet coat flows under surface tension, which smooths the short wavelengths first. The model applies that as a low-pass filter of width σ (three box passes approximating a Gaussian), then adds the film's own low-frequency orange peel. It is shaded with GGX at the topcoat roughness α, and each device pixel averages 4×4 samples, so detail finer than a pixel shows up as roughness in the sheen rather than as visible relief.

Fast kernel. The kernel places sparse compact dents on a hashed grid, sized like the levelled craters, and adds up their analytic slopes. It needs no rows and no neighbouring pixels, so any pixel can be computed on its own. The fit adjusts the dent amplitude and an effective sheen roughness until the kernel's mean luminance and band spread match the reference.
